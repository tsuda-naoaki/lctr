Grounds of Establishment

Grounds for Establishing the Results

Results 1–8, 12–14, 17, and 18 rest on the source definitions, distinctions, statement of possibility, and mechanical arguments. For Results 9–11, the source invokes natural-day inequality, astronomical correction, pendulum-clock experiments, and eclipses of Jupiter’s satellites. Evidence Hierarchy

Directly Measured Objects

The astronomical-correction paragraph invokes natural-day inequality, pendulum-clock experiments, and eclipses of Jupiter’s satellites. The bucket experiment observes water-surface shape in relation to the motions of vessel and water. Cord tension in the two-globe argument is a comparison quantity posited for two globes at a fixed separation.

Directly Measured Predicates

The source states the inequality of natural-day lengths and cites clock experiments and eclipses as evidence that correction is necessary to determine phenomenon times. For the bucket, it describes the rise and concavity of the water surface as rotation is transferred to the water, together with the eventual equality of revolution periods at relative rest with respect to the vessel.

Conditions and Domain of Application of Direct Measurement

Evidence for astronomical correction concerns use of natural days as a measure, clock experiments, and comparison with satellite eclipses. Bucket observations concern a procedure starting with vessel and water at rest, rotating the vessel, and transferring motion to the water. The inter-globe separation, circular motion about a common center of gravity, equal forces applied at once, and changes in tension are conditions of the two-globe thought experiment.

Results concerning absolute and relative time, absolute and relative motion, the order of the parts of time, quantity of motion, force, and the laws of motion apply to the bodies, motions, and mechanical systems specified by the definitions and laws in the original source.

Evidence-Effect Domain

The formal evidence in this reference directly supports the 18 results in the adopted audit domain, astronomical observations and corrections, clock comparisons, the shape of the water surface in the bucket, the argument using two globes and cord tension, and dependence on the laws of motion.

Evidence-Effect Boundary

The evidence effect of the astronomical observations, clock comparisons, water-surface shape, and tension extends to the respective physical bearers, observational relations, comparison differences, and mechanical derivations. The transition from those items to the intrinsic nature and uniform progress of absolute time and to physical-time qualification independent of external objects lies beyond the evidence-effect boundary under Definition 7.115.

Result-Preservation Domain

Result-Preservation Domain

The mathematical, observational, and mechanical content of results 6, 8–13, 17, and 18 is preserved when the time presuppositions used there are supplied by the corresponding generated time contents in the LCTR generation sequence and the original source’s physical inputs, observations, correction rules, and directions of dependence are retained. The content concerning measure relations, relative-motion relations, water-surface shape, and tension in results 5, 7, and 14–16 is preserved under the same conditions, but attribution to primitive absolute time or absolute motion falls outside the preservation domain. The declaration that time is undefined, the intrinsic nature and uniform progress of absolute time, and its independence from external objects in results 1–4 are not preserved as results satisfying LCTR physical-time qualification.

Placement of Grounding Obligations

Placement of Grounding Obligations

The 41 time contents audited in this reference are not generated from non-temporal physical inputs before use; they are accepted as already established time inputs. The judgment on the placement of the physical-time grounding obligation is “backgrounded by primitive time input.” This judgment is grounded in a comparison between the use of each time content in a law operation or result derivation and the absence, before that use, of the corresponding generation or establishment condition as an input, formation condition, evaluation condition, or invalidation condition.

Evidence of Actual Invalidation

Evidence of Actual Invalidation

The complete-generation judgment and remaining-root judgment concern physical-time qualification and are distinct from the actual invalidation of a specific condition that determines the value or domain of application of a result. Neither the original-source formal evidence nor the LCTR audit evidence for this reference establishes the actual invalidation of such a target condition. The actual-invalidation judgment is “indeterminate from the original-source evidence”; actual invalidation is not inferred solely from the backgrounding of a grounding obligation.

Downstream Reach

Downstream Reach

The reach to results that depend on primitive time roots is determined for this reference by the primitive-time-root–result dependence relations and minimal primitive-time-root supports reconstructed in this division. This is structural reach. Because the actual invalidation of a specific condition has not been established, the judgment on actual reach from that invalidation position to results is “indeterminate from the original-source evidence.” Structural reach does not substitute for actual reach.

Repair Scope

Repair Scope

On the basis of structural reach, the targets of repair are the corresponding time content and the generation or establishment conditions required before its use. The scope of recalculation is limited to results for which actual invalidation has been independently established and that are reached by dependence from the invalidation position. Because actual invalidation is indeterminate in this reference, this repair-and-recalculation specification is conditional. If the condition holds, the target results are regenerated or recalculated from the repaired inputs, or their validity domain is restricted.

Individual Statements

Statement 1: Absolute, True, and Mathematical Time and Duration

Verbatim Original

Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature flows equably without regard to anything external, and by another name is called duration :

Original-Source Location

Newton, The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy, Motte translation, Definitions, Scholium I, source p. 77.

Minimal Context

The same Scholium distinguishes time, space, place, and motion as absolute and relative, true and apparent, and mathematical and common. The quoted passage is followed immediately by the description of relative, apparent, and common time.

Statement 2: Relative, Apparent, and Common Time

Verbatim Original

relative, apparent, and common time, is some sensible and external (whether accurate or unequable) measure of duration by the means of motion, which is commonly used instead of true time; such as an hour, a day, a month, a year.

Original-Source Location

Newton, The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy, Motte translation, Definitions, Scholium I, source p. 77.

Minimal Context

The passage follows the description that also calls absolute, true, and mathematical time duration, and describes relative time as an external measure by motion. The subsequent passage distinguishes absolute from relative space.

Statement 3: Possible Nonexistence of Uniform Motion as a Measure of Time

Verbatim Original

It may be, that there is no such thing as an equable motion, whereby time may be accurately measured. All motions may be accelerated and retarded, but the true, or equable, progress of absolute time is liable to no change.

Original-Source Location

Newton, The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy, Motte translation, the Scholium following the Definitions, the astronomical-correction paragraph after item IV, source pp. 78–79.

Minimal Context

The passage follows the description of the inequality of natural days and their correction by astronomers. The same paragraph proceeds to distinguish the duration of the existence of things from motion.

Statement 4: Duration Independent of Motion

Verbatim Original

The duration or perseverance of the existence of things remains the same, whether the motions are swift or slow, or none at all: and therefore it ought to be distinguished from what are only sensible measures thereof; […]

Original-Source Location

Newton, The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy, Motte translation, the Scholium following the Definitions, the astronomical-correction paragraph after item IV, source p. 79.

Minimal Context

The passage is placed immediately after the possible nonexistence of a uniform measuring motion and the uniform progress of absolute time. The subsequent passage refers to the astronomical equation of time, pendulum clocks, and eclipses of Jupiter’s satellites.

Common Evidence with Domain of Application

The following four statements are attributed to Newton’s Scholium, and their quoted English wording is attributed to Motte’s translation.

Individual Audit

Interpretive Possibilities

The first statement describes time as flowing uniformly by its own nature independently of external objects. It admits a reading as a property of physical reality and a reading as a mathematical standard distinguished from empirical measures. In the second statement, being a sensible external measure of duration by motion and being commonly used in place of true time are both predicated of the same relative time. The third contrasts the possible nonexistence of equable motion permitting accurate measurement with the equable progress of absolute time, which remains unaffected by changes in motion. The fourth states both the independence of duration from motion and the distinction between duration itself and its sensible measures.

Independent Grounds

The four statements belong to the treatment of time, duration, and measures in the Scholium following the Definitions. The astronomical-correction paragraph states the inequality of natural days and invokes pendulum-clock experiments and eclipses of Jupiter’s satellites to establish the need for correction. These observational grounds concern relative time measures and the determination of phenomenon times.

Evidence-Effect Boundary

The first statement directly supports the attribution to absolute time of an intrinsic nature, uniform flow, external independence, and the name duration. The second statement directly supports the statement that relative time is an external measure of duration by motion and that hours, days, months, and years are examples of that measure. The third statement directly supports the possible nonexistence of a perfect measure of time by uniform motion and the progress of absolute time said to remain invariant under acceleration and retardation of motions. The fourth statement directly supports the statement that the duration of things is independent of their state of motion and is distinguished from sensible measures. None of the four statements provides a path that generates the time content used in descriptions of absolute time, duration, relative-time measures, or measuring motions solely from non-temporal physical inputs. Nor do the definitions, distinctions, and relations given by the four statements alone establish the physical-time qualification of that time content.

Corresponding Formal Evidence

The first statement corresponds to time contents 1–6 in Definition S.12, input claims 4–6 in Definition S.13, and results 3–4 in Definition S.15. The second statement corresponds to time contents 7–12, input claims 7–9, and results 5–6. The third statement corresponds to Time Contents 1–9 and 27–29, Claims 21–22, and result 12. The fourth statement corresponds to time contents 5–6, input claim 23, and the distinction between duration and sensible measures in the same reference.

Within-Reference Comparison

The first and fourth statements describe time or duration as independent of external objects or states of motion; the second statement takes sensible measures by motion as proxy indications of it; and the third statement states the possible nonexistence of a perfectly uniform measuring motion. Within the same reference, the ontological positing of the quantity measured is distinguished from the empirical construction of the measuring scale, while no physical generation path connecting the two is given.
