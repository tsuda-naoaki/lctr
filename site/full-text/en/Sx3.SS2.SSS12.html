Grounds of Establishment

Grounds of Establishment of the Results

The evidence types directly supplied by this reference are textbook definitions, mathematical derivations, explanations of measurement procedures, conceptual arguments, and reports of existing experimental results.

  1. 1.

    The heartbeat example assumes equal intervals, and the pendulum uses equal periods at small amplitude. Each clock standard rests on its calibration and comparison of regularity.

  2. 2.

    Apparently periodic repetition grounds time measurement, and consistency between different procedures is examined. The source poses questions about proving true periodicity and about the meaning of still shorter times.

  3. 3.

    Lifetime estimation from flight distance presupposes speed; long-time estimation from natural markers presupposes formation periods; radioactive dating presupposes a half-life and material history. Radar ranging uses round-trip time, stellar parallax uses observation times separated by seasons, and a light-year uses one year and light speed. Cosmic age is stated as an estimate current at the time of the source.

  4. 4.

    The Galilean transformation uses common time; the Lorentz transformation uses subluminal relative motion in the specified direction and invariant light speed. The source describes experiments supporting relativity for uniform translation and treats rotation as detectable. Its discussion of removing the stars is an untested question.

  5. 5.

    The constituents are the round-trip calculation in the hypothetical model, the reported null result, and the model explanation by length contraction.

  6. 6.

    The light-clock construction and the relativity principle yield a common rate, which applies to processes governed by the same physical laws. Application to cancer carries the source qualification “nearly certain.”

  7. 7.

    Chapter 15 reports agreement between moving-muon lifetimes and prediction. Chapter 16 describes observations involving curved motion and presents the comparison of a particle returning after a complete circle with a stationary particle as a prediction for an experiment not yet performed.

  8. 8.

    The specified frame, light-propagation conditions, clock readings and midpoint convention determine remote times. Distant simultaneity depends on the frame.

  9. 9.

    Elapsed ages are compared for common departure and reunion events and specified stay-at-home and outward-and-return paths between them. Turnaround and acceleration are features of these paths.

  10. 10.

    The statement connecting time-translation invariance to energy conservation adds the principles of quantum mechanics. The differential derivation of relativistic mass uses E=m⁢c2E=mc^{2}, the rate of work and the time derivative of momentum. The collision derivation uses elastic scattering of identical particles, conservation laws, parallel momentum and velocity, and the low-speed limit.

  11. 11.

    Event positions and times, together with the temporal correspondence of the same particle, describe a worldline. Interval-sign classification and the signal-speed restriction determine causal domains of possible influence.

  12. 12.

    In the source’s c=1c=1 units, energy follows the same transformation law as the time component of a four-vector. Energy-momentum transformations use the existing velocity and coordinate transformations and dynamical definitions, yielding the four-component conservation relation.

  13. 13.

    E=h⁢νE=h\nu, p=h/λp=h/\lambda and ν=c/λ\nu=c/\lambda relate photon energy, momentum and frequency. Given the momentum direction and sign convention for the coordinate transformation, the energy transformation yields the Doppler frequency.

  14. 14.

    The source relation expresses the minimum error in time measurement from the energy uncertainty of the process whose time interval is measured. The meaning of the time interval as the measurand is given; this specification holds even when its numerical value is unknown.

Individual Statements

Objects of the Statements

The definability and measurement of time, dating using a half-life, the age and beginning of the universe and what precedes it, the common rate of moving clocks and the expression that time itself appears slower, and application to heartbeat, thought and cancer development.

Verbatim Original (English)

time is one of the things we probably cannot define

how we measure it

In an arbitrary time interval tt there are t/Tt/T “half-lives,”

At some time more than five billion years ago, the universe started. It is now believed that at least our part of the universe had its beginning about ten or twelve billion years ago. We do not know what happened before then. In fact, we may well ask again: Does the question make any sense? Does an earlier time have any meaning?

Now if all moving clocks run slower, if no way of measuring time gives anything but a slower rate, we shall just have to say, in a certain sense, that time itself appears to be slower in a space ship.

the man’s pulse rate, his thought processes

Original-Source Locations

The first and second quotations are from Feynman, Leighton, and Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I, Chapter 5, §5-2, Time. The third quotation is from Chapter 5, §5-4, Long times, in the same book. The fourth quotation is from the end of Chapter 5, §5-4, Long times, in the same book. The fifth through seventh quotations are from the same paragraph in Chapter 15, §15-4, Transformation of time, in the same book.

Minimal Context Required for Interpretation

Section 5-2 describes the difficulty of defining time in a dictionary and explains methods of measurement. It bases the definition of time on apparently periodic repetition. Section 5-4 obtains the unknown age tt from the initial amount AA, present amount BB and half-life TT, connecting the discussion to the age and beginning of the universe and the meaning of what precedes that beginning. Under the relativity principle and common physical laws, Section 15-4 extends the light-clock rate relation to other clocks and processes, describing the slowness of time itself with the qualifications “in a certain sense” and “appears.” It mentions heartbeat, thought, growth, aging and cancer development, describing the application to cancer as “nearly certain.”

Attribution of the Statements

All seven statements are attributable to the text of Volume I by Feynman, Leighton, and Sands.

Contents Directly Stated by the Quotations

The first quotation identifies time as one of the things that we, including the authors, probably cannot define in the dictionary sense. The second makes the method of measuring time the subject of discussion. The third quotation expresses the number of half-lives contained in an arbitrary time interval tt as t/Tt/T. The fourth quotation assigns a beginning and an age to the universe and to our part of the universe, and asks about events and the meaning of time before that beginning. The fifth quotation states that, if all moving clocks and all methods of measuring time give a slower rate, time itself appears slower in the spacecraft in a certain sense. The sixth and seventh quotations extend the object of application of that rate difference to human biological and cognitive processes.

Explicit Domains of Application of the Statements

Time measurement, radioactive dating, descriptions of the age of the universe, special-relativistic clock comparison, moving-particle lifetimes, and human biological and cognitive processes.

Evidence Provenance

The text of Feynman, Leighton and Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I, Chapter 5, §§5-2 and 5-4, and Chapter 15, §15-4.

Individual Audit

Interpretability

The first and second statements describe the difficulty of a dictionary definition and explain measurement methods. Apparently periodic repetition provides the basis for measuring and defining time. The third states the ratio of an arbitrary interval tt to the half-life TT; dating obtains tt from the initial amount, present amount and half-life. The fourth follows its account of cosmic age with a question about the meaning of time before the beginning. The fifth statement, that time itself appears slower, occurs within the argument that clocks and processes share a common rate under the relativity principle and carries explicit qualifications. The sixth and seventh apply the argument to biological and cognitive processes governed by the same physical laws, with the qualification “nearly certain” for cancer.

Independent Grounds

The grounds for the seven statements are explanations of measurement methods, calculation using the decay law, an account of cosmic age, and the rate argument based on the relativity principle and common physical laws. The muon measurements reported in the same chapter compare the rest mean lifetime with the laboratory-frame lifetime. Application to biological processes is presented as theoretical inference from common physical laws.

Evidence-Effect Boundary

Measurement methods presuppose periods and their comparison; dating presupposes the time dependence of decay and a half-life; relativistic rate relations presuppose times, elapsed times and velocities. The account of cosmic age introduces a beginning and the temporal relation of being earlier. Physical use of these contents requires the corresponding generation structure to hold. The evidence-effect boundary of Definition 7.115 lies at the passage from the measurement, calculation or explanation supported by each statement to that establishment.
