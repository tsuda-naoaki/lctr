Formal Objects

The audit uses 25 time contents, 45 claims, 11 transfers and 20 results. The source definitions, operations and hypotheses are specified in the respective claims. Penrose assesses the singularity theorems as indicating limits of known physical laws and looks to singular structure for the origin of the arrow of time (p. 581). Section 12.2 lists seven arrows of time, or possible arrows. Microscopic neutral-K-meson asymmetry, quantum observation, thermodynamics, radiation, psychological time, cosmic expansion, and black-hole/white-hole processes are treated as distinct questions.

Penrose discusses the reality of the wave function and observational state reduction, seeking new laws that incorporate observation. For the subsequent discussion in Section 12.2, he provisionally adopts the conventional view that quantum mechanics has no manifest arrow of time, returning to measurement in Section 12.4 (pp. 583–586, 635–636). His treatment of psychological time distinguishes an observer-independent universal present, branching futures, ease of prediction and retrodiction, and fixity of the past, before returning to conventional Hausdorff spacetime (pp. 591–596).

The initial Weyl condition is Penrose’s proposal. He prefers the form in which Weyl curvature tends to zero, while also mentioning faster decay, boundedness and domination by Ricci curvature; he has not examined their differences in detail (pp. 630–631). He states that detailed initial matter fluctuations require further understanding of particle physics (p. 634).

Penrose presents a cosmological account using an initial restriction and randomness within it. Against explaining the entropy imbalance of the entire universe by a large fluctuation favorable to observers, he argues that producing a few observers would require a smaller entropy decrease (pp. 633–634). Section 12.4 outlines prospects involving nonlinear quantum gravity incorporating observation, baryon–antibaryon production and annihilation, and detection of CPT violation. The logarithmic nonlinear Schrödinger model he cites is time-symmetric (pp. 635–638).

Assumptions

The quantum comparisons use unitary evolution and specified observation times. The thermodynamic, radiative and psychological arguments add a low-entropy past condition to time-symmetric laws. Classical black-hole/white-hole reversal uses time-symmetric equations of state; area nondecrease uses the conditions of the classical area theorem. The causal-boundary definitions and equivalences assume strong causality. Application of strong cosmic censorship to physical spacetimes is Penrose’s conjecture. The initial Weyl restriction is an additional condition. For history spaces, Penrose uses classical deterministic Hamiltonian evolution and coarse-graining, setting aside infinite-dimensional, infinite-measure and general-relativistic gauge problems (pp. 631–632).

Individual Statements

Statement Evidence 1

Verbatim Original (English):

The local physical laws we know and understand are all symmetrical in time. Yet on a macroscopic level, time-asymmetry is manifest.

Original-source location: Penrose, Singularities and Time-Asymmetry, p. 582; Section 12.2. Minimal context: Immediately after contrasting time-symmetric local laws with macroscopic asymmetry, Penrose mentions the neutral-K-meson exception and the unresolved question of quantum observation. He then lists seven arrows of time, or possible arrows. Attribution: Roger Penrose.

Statement Evidence 2

Verbatim Original (English):

Indeed, these laws are even self-limiting, as the singularity theorems6,7,8 seem to show. But I do not feel that this is a cause for pessimism. There is a need for new laws in any case; while, in my opinion, the presence and the apparent structure of spacetime singularities contain the key to the solution to one of the long-standing mysteries of physics: the origin of the arrow of time.

Original-source location: Penrose, Singularities and Time-Asymmetry, p. 581; Section 12.1. Minimal context: Penrose gives his assessment of the singularity theorems and his own view that singular structure holds the key to the origin of the arrow of time. Attribution: Roger Penrose.

According to section 12.2.6, some low-entropy assumption does need to be imposed on the big bang; that is, the mere fact that the universe expands away from a singularity is in no way sufficient.

Minimal context: Following the expansion/recollapse comparison in Section 12.2.6, Penrose states that explaining thermodynamic direction requires a low-entropy past condition in addition to the fact of expansion. Common Evidence with Specified Domain of Application

Individual Audits

First Statement

Penrose contrasts time-symmetric local laws with macroscopic asymmetry and introduces seven arrows or candidates. Penrose draws the comparison from known laws, neutral-K-meson experiments and macroscopic phenomena, and explicitly identifies the K-meson exception and unresolved quantum-observation question in that context. The corresponding formal evidence comprises Time Contents 1, 3–5, 7–13 and 15; Claims 6–8, 10–11, 15–19 and 24; and Results 1–7.

Second Statement

Penrose states his own assessment of the singularity theorems and his research proposal seeking the origin of the arrow of time in singular structure. His assessment of application limits rests on the classical spacetime theorems he cites, while his view of the origin rests on a theoretical interpretation of singular structure. The corresponding formal evidence comprises Time Contents 2 and 14 and Claim 5.

Third Statement

Penrose argues that a low-entropy past condition is required in addition to cosmic expansion. His grounds are the comparison of a recollapsing universe, an observer inside a black hole and thermodynamic direction in Section 12.2.6, and its synthesis at the beginning of Section 12.3. The corresponding formal evidence comprises Time Contents 1–2, 6–8 and 13–15; Claims 4, 11–13, 18–19 and 24; and Results 3 and 6.
