The earlier time-generation theories examined by LCTR use temporal quantities or relations that have not yet been generated in their derivations of time. Interpretations that treat operators, relative configurations, or states as nontemporal starting points overlook temporal dependencies within those descriptions.

Existing physics also places time among the premises of physical-law descriptions and leaves the conditions establishing that time in the background of individual fields. LCTR formulates this presupposed use as the problem of “primitive time” and treats the generation of time and its conditions of use within the same construction framework.

1. Time symbols and the content they represent

LCTR examines the physical quantities and relations that a theory uses as time, together with their grounds. After a theory removes the external time symbol t from an equation, its derivation can still depend on proper time, clock rates, time intervals, or physical succession.

A theory that selects a variable as a clock must establish the conditions under which that variable can serve as time. A theory that derives time from causal order must provide a construction from that order to clock scales, time intervals, and time variables usable in physical laws. LCTR audits the temporal content each construction generates and the temporal content it retains as a premise.

2. What is primitive-time input?

When a theory uses temporal content in calculations or in deriving results without a complete path establishing that content from nontemporal physical inputs, LCTR calls that content “primitive-time input”.

LCTR traces dependencies from the final expression of a proposed time-emergence theory back to its inputs. It examines the temporal content required by intermediate operations, comparisons, clock interpretations, and uses of results, and checks the construction path for each. If a derivation of one temporal representation depends on other temporal content that has not been generated, the theory must also generate that content.

3. Four examples from the supplement audit

An interpretation that classifies inputs as nontemporal solely because a description lacks an external time variable or uses operators, relative configurations, or states overlooks time required in derivations and uses of results. The LCTR supplement identifies the temporal dependencies involved in this misinterpretation.

DeWitt (1967): clock operators and proper time

DeWitt constructs model-clock proper time from phase and angular frequency and treats clock operators and conditional evolution. The model-clock construction uses the proper-time derivative in source Eq. (7.4) and angular frequency from Eqs. (7.57)–(7.59). The inputs to the operator representation of the clock retain proper-time derivatives and angular frequency.

Barbour (1994 I): relative configurations and their realized history

Barbour describes a timeless kinematics based on the complete relative configuration of the universe. The source integral defining an ephemeris-time difference uses a realized history of configurations and its direction. Comparisons between subsystems require agreement of clock rates, and the specialization to general relativity recovers local proper time. LCTR identifies the physical succession, clock rates, and proper-time correspondence used in this construction as temporal inputs. Recovery from relative configurations depends on the history connecting those configurations and its temporal use.

Supplement S.2.33, Individual Audit (printed p. 1164) · Source 33: bibliography

Connes–Rovelli (1994): thermal time and established time scales

Connes and Rovelli postulate the use of a state-dependent flow as physical time. The modular construction yields an outer-automorphism class on a specified algebra and a self-KMS relation. In connecting the construction to physical scales, the Gibbs and Rindler branches compare scales using established time. LCTR separates the construction of the flow from the inputs to its comparison with established time and identifies the temporal dependencies retained in that comparison.

Supplement S.2.35, Individual Audit (printed p. 1197) · Source 35: bibliography

Kiefer (2009): equations without external time and semiclassical time

Kiefer argues for fundamental timelessness using the Wheeler–DeWitt description without an external time parameter and treats semiclassical time through subsystem separation and approximation. LCTR separately audits the whole-system equation’s independence from external time, the evolution parameter introduced for a subsystem, and the identification of a time direction. The source’s numerical evaluation of interference suppression in Eq. (6) also uses the Hubble constant H₀ among its inputs. Treating the absence of external time in an equation as proof of nontemporal inputs throughout the approximations and numerical evaluations overlooks these uses of time.

4. Why primitive time was overlooked

Existing physics uses time as a premise in descriptions of motion, measurement, synchronization, coordinates, and spacetime structure. Mechanics uses a time parameter; relativity treats coordinate time, proper time, and comparisons between worldlines; quantum theory treats observational coupling and state identification; time metrology and GPS treat clock adjustments and corrections. Each field incorporates premises for time use into its formulations, while the construction of those premises recedes into the background.

The primitive-time problem posed by LCTR requires identifying each item of temporal content used by a theory and tracing its grounds back to nontemporal physical inputs. Existing formulations lack a framework that treats this problem as a construction requirement across their uses of time. The recovery of a local temporal representation or a successful calculation can obscure the unconstructed time in its premises.

In Volume I, Chapter 5 of The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Feynman writes that “we”, including himself, probably cannot define time in the dictionary sense, then turns to its measurement. Section 1.6 of LCTR argues that, even after this candid statement, physics did not take up the lack of a definition of time itself as a construction problem, and continued to leave its presupposed use of time in the background of individual fields. LCTR makes the conditions establishing time reference, prior to measurement, the object of construction.

5. What did the 72-source audit find?

LCTR audited 72 representative sources spanning theories, experiments, metrological standards, and engineering, from antiquity to the present. All 72 contain primitive-time input. The number of sources that generate all the temporal content they use from nontemporal physical inputs is zero.

The LCTR audit compares each source’s mathematical expressions with its statements about time. It identifies what is constructed, what remains presupposed, and which results depend on those presuppositions. The source list identifies each work; Chapter 7 and the supplement provide the detailed audits.

6. What did LCTR change?

LCTR provides the first complete construction of physical time from nontemporal physical inputs alone. Starting from local indications, retained records, differences in object states, and information coupling, it constructs temporal comparisons, temporal order, time representations, and time variables usable in physical laws. At no stage does the construction presuppose temporal quantities or relations that it has not yet generated.

LCTR specifies the conditions at each stage, from the generation of time to continuous approximation and differential formulation. Its audit follows the construction dependencies, checks those conditions, and establishes where temporal representations and results can be used. Where conditions fail to hold, it identifies those conditions and the temporal representations or results that lack qualification for use.

7. What happens to existing results?

LCTR preserves existing results within the scope directly supported by measurement, prediction, model fitting, or computation. It generates temporal content and its physical grounds, audits validity domains and failure locations, and establishes the conditions for safe reuse.

LCTR assesses what each experiment or calculation directly supports and how gaps in time generation affect it. Conditions, evidence, and dependencies determine which results can be retained, which constructions require repair, and which objects require regeneration or recomputation.
